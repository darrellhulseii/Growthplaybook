Section 01

Marketing

Get attention and demand without ever mentioning money. Nine steps, run in order.

The positioning, in one line: sell control, not income. You are legally barred from earnings claims and every competitor leads with money anyway. The research says pay is the second reason people leave a job, behind lack of development and uncaring leadership - so you are being forced to lead with the thing that actually works.

M1

Lock the four pillars into every piece of copy

  • You decide when you work. "Nobody assigns you a shift."
  • Advancement is not a title. "You get better at a skill you keep."
  • You will not be managed by a stranger. "The person who trains you knocks with you."
  • We tell you what this is. "Commission only. No floor. Plenty of people decide it is not for them."

Never say: an implied schedule, a title ladder, hype culture, urgency or limited spots. Pillar 4 is the whole strategy - honesty is your screening mechanism. Every competitor optimizes for applicant volume. You optimize for fit.

Done whenYour site, your DMs, your reel captions and your rep channel all use the same four sentences, and you can point at the sentence in each place.
M2

Call every install by hand at day 7

  • Not a survey. A conversation. Three questions: why did you say yes, what almost stopped you, who else on this street needs this.
  • Write the answers down verbatim. Their words, not your paraphrase.
  • Run this for eight weeks minimum before you automate anything.

This is the single highest-leverage unscalable thing available to you. It produces your objection list, your referral list and your content calendar at the same time - and it is the one input every serious operator in the source material independently landed on.

Done whenYou have a written objection list built from real calls, not from a training deck.
M3

Turn the objection list into your copy

  • The line a homeowner used about why they almost did not buy becomes your next reel script, word for word.
  • Rank objections by frequency. The top three get answered in the first four reels.
  • Same list goes to reps as the standard rebuttal sheet.
Done whenYour top three objections each have a written answer that a new rep can read out loud at a door.
M4

Automate day 7 and day 90 - only now

  • Day 7 SMS: catches install problems before they turn into cancellations.
  • Day 90 SMS: asks for the review and the referral at peak happiness.
  • Measure cancellation rate before and after. This is the first number that should visibly move.

Your specific trap: with your tooling skill the instinct is to build the automation first. Manual before automated, every time.

Done whenCancellation rate is measurably lower than your pre-automation baseline, and you can show the two numbers.
M5

Pick one series banner and never change it

  • Recommended: "Inside a Real Door-to-Door Agency:" - documentary framing, zero promises.
  • Fixed elements, every single post: black banner bar top 12 percent, EP badge, you talking on top half, screen share on bottom half, 2-4 word captions with one word stressed, CTA burned into the low third.
  • 30-50 seconds. Start mid-sentence. No greeting, no intro, no name.
  • Bottom half is your unfair advantage - real mapping, real ledger, real routes. Nobody else in D2D recruiting shows actual tooling.
Done whenSomeone who has seen your fifth post recognizes the sixth at a glance.

M8

Launch the Creator Corps

  • Audit your own roster first. Who already posts anything? Start there, not with strangers. Ignore follower count - willingness to film the actual day is the only qualifier.
  • One-page content addendum: voluntary participation, usage rights to you, claim rules listed plainly.
  • Give them the raw material every Monday - one hook, one caption, one example. Removing "what do I post" is most of the job.
  • Pay on outcome: a referral bonus when someone they sourced completes a ride-along and onboards.
  • Never require posting. Required content from a contractor is a control signal against your 1099 classification.
  • Let everything run organically first, then whitelist only what already performs. Kill anything flat in 72 hours.

One owner posting three times a week loses to fifteen reps posting twice - and the rep footage converts better, because a recruit trusts a rep over an owner.

Done whenAt least five reps post in a month without being chased, and you have reposted a named winner every Thursday for four weeks.

  • Standing disclosure on episode 1 and any time the number appears: it is company gross revenue, not profit, not your paycheck, not what a contractor earns.
  • Channel B - Complete Home Today. Recruiting. No figure, no range, no top-rep example, ever.

  • No income claims. Not a figure, not a range, not a top-rep example, not a screenshot. Not in the banner, caption, DM or comments.
  • No employment language. Never salary, hourly, benefits, hiring, employee or manager.
  • Do not script a schedule on camera. Content showing a required start time undercuts the classification.
  • Carrier marks and offers. Follow dealer marketing rules. Never burn a price into a reel that lives for months.
  • License display. Where security is shown or discussed, display CA ACO #6446.
  • Blur customer data. Any screen share touching addresses, names or order details gets masked.
  • Reposting is adoption. Never repost a rep post that breaks a rule - it becomes your post.

Section 02

Business

The operating layer. How you run the company so it does not collapse under its own growth.

Marketing is the demand engine. This is the production engine. At scale, this company is not a sales team - it is a system that manufactures productive reps. Run these nine steps continuously, not once.

B1

Run the algorithm, in order, on one system per quarter

Five steps, and the order is the entire point. The costly mistake is optimizing, accelerating and automating a part that turned out not to need to exist.

  • 1. Make the requirements less dumb. Put a person's name next to every rule. If nobody will own a rule, it is not a rule. "An office needs a lease." "Reps need a manager." "Recruiting needs an interview." All inherited, none reasoned.
  • 2. Delete the part or the process. If you are not forced to put back 10 percent of what you deleted, you did not delete enough.
  • 3. Simplify what survives. Third for a reason - most people optimize things that should not exist.
  • 4. Accelerate cycle time. If you are digging your grave, do not dig it faster.
  • 5. Automate, last. Never first.

Your starting delete list: the daily morning meeting, the title ladder, the screening phone call, the applicant interview, the office lease, paper commission statements. Expect to re-add roughly one - likely a single weekly team touchpoint, not daily and not mandatory.

Done whenYou keep a written record of what you deleted each week and whether you had to add it back. That record is the log of what you chose.
B2

Run one scoreboard - six numbers, one diagnosis

  • Active reps
  • Units installed per month
  • Day-90 survival percent
  • Ride-alongs per month
  • Cells operating
  • Playbook written - yes or no

The scoreboard forecasts nothing. Its only job is to name the single binding constraint so you stop working on the other five. Distrust every number that stands in for the thing you actually care about: applications are a proxy for recruits, posts published are a proxy for pipeline, units sold are a proxy for units that survive ninety days.

Done whenYou review it every Friday and can name this week's binding constraint in one sentence.
B3

Publish one commission schedule and hold to it

  • One schedule. One override level, corporate to cell. No discretionary haircut, ever.
  • Holdback rules and chargeback terms visible before anyone signs.
  • The ledger is the statement. No paper commission statements.
Done whenA rep can read the schedule, predict their own payout, and be right.
B4

Size the reserve for the cell count you will have in twelve months

  • Track three separate numbers, never one: units written, units installed, units surviving day 90. Only the third one is real revenue.
  • Holdback and chargeback timing is the failure mode that arrives exactly when cell count outruns reserve policy.
  • Surplus is not the reward, it is a responsibility with a job to do. Here that job is reserve and reinvestment.

Knowing your own numbers honestly was a competitive advantage in 1870 and it still is - most D2D operators genuinely do not know their chargeback rate.

Done whenYou can state your chargeback rate and your day-90 survival rate from memory, and the reserve is set against next year's headcount.
B5

Write the back office down end to end

  • Licensing, payouts, compliance, mapping, recruiting funnel, training, onboarding path.
  • Written so a stranger could run a cell from it without calling you.
  • Build it while you are the only user. It becomes the product you license later - this is literally what an operator pays override for.
Done whenSomeone who has never met you runs a full week off the document and does not phone you once.
B6

Open the referral program

  • Paid on installed and surviving accounts only. Never on a signature.
  • Sources in order: past customers first, then property managers, HOA contacts, and the trades already inside the home.
  • Your referral network is not something you go find - it is sitting in your install list right now. Phase 1 and this step are the same list, worked twice.

A referred sale and a knocked sale carry the same revenue at very different cost. The margin gap understates it: a referral does not consume a rep-day, which is the actual scarce resource, and it closes faster and cancels less.

Done whenA measurable share of monthly sales arrives without consuming a rep-day, and you can name that share.

B8

Convert commission income into an owned asset

  • Now: ADT monitoring RMR. It does not disappear when a fiber dealer agreement changes.
  • Then: the water filter replacement cycle, which compounds on every installed system every 12-18 months.
  • Eventually: passings you own. Coverage is no longer the industry's constraint - penetration is. The scarce asset in fiber right now is a functioning ground game, and that is precisely what you are building.
  • Entry order if you ever go there: retail ISP on an open-access network first, then MDU builds, then acquiring a stalled network or greenfield. Skipping to greenfield is how people lose everything they built in the first nine years.

The pattern is two centuries old. Astor did not get rich by being better at fur - he used the fur trade to buy the appreciating asset underneath the city.

Done whenA meaningful and growing share of monthly revenue arrives whether or not anyone knocked that month.
B9

Keep the compliance surface off the chopping block

  • Licensing, cooling-off rights, worker classification and earnings-claim rules are not dumb requirements to be deleted. Run the algorithm on processes, never on compliance.
  • 1099 posture: you cannot demand hours. Intensity is modeled, never required. Every control signal compounds across the network.
  • Security: CA ACO #6446, ACE #552544, plus individual agent registration. Thirty-seven states license alarm work, and requirements vary sharply by state.
  • Water, when it comes: three-day cooling-off, contract-language rules for sales negotiated in Spanish and several other languages, CSLB classification for installs, device certification, financing regulations, senior protections, and health claims - say only what the certification says.
  • Before Gate 3, retain counsel on franchise and business-opportunity law. A licensed operator network with a fee, a marketing plan and a shared brand can meet the legal definition of a franchise.
Done whenYou have a named person and a calendar date for every licence renewal and every dealer marketing rule.

Three ways this dies - watch these, not the top line

  • Holdback and chargeback timing at the moment cell count outruns reserve policy.
  • Carrier concentration while Frontier is your only meaningful line under new ownership. Treat terms as unsettled.
  • Override revenue that starts depending on new cells opening rather than existing cells producing. If that inverts you will not see it in the top line - watch per-cell production.
Section 03

Office Growth

How one cell becomes many without becoming the thing you are trying to replace.

Nobody reaches a large office count by opening them one at a time with their own capital. Company-owned tops out around 10-25 offices before the org chart eats you. The licensed owner-operator network is the only shape that scales - and the shape is not the problem.

What actually makes a network predatory - only three things

  • An operator who earns more from recruiting than from selling.
  • A promotion ladder that requires a downline to advance.
  • An entry fee.

Remove those three and you have a distribution network. Keep any one and you have rebuilt the thing you set out to replace.

  • Operator quality collapse at Gate 4.
  • Recruiting-funded growth. If override depends on new cells opening rather than existing cells producing, the model has inverted.
  • Classification exposure at scale. Two thousand contractors is a much bigger target than twenty.

Coverage ratio is onboards divided by the replacements you need to stay flat. Below 2x you are shrinking even if sales look fine. Planning arithmetic only - none of these figures belong in recruiting material.

R1

Go always-on. Never blitz.

  • Post weekly and book ride-alongs weekly whether or not you need bodies.
  • A blitz fills a class, then leaves you with nothing three weeks later when half of them quit.
  • A pipeline built during an emergency is already too late. Consistency is the whole mechanism.
Done whenYou have run twelve straight weeks of recruiting activity, including the weeks you were full.
R2

Delete the interview and the screening call

  • The DM sequence screens better, and asynchronously.
  • The ride-along is the conversion event. Half a day shadowing a rep tests the actual job, and filters harder than any interview ever did.
  • No commitment either side. That framing is what makes people show up.
Done whenNobody has been interviewed in a month and your onboard quality did not drop.
R3

Tell them what it is, before they spend time on it

  • 1099 independent contractor. Commission only. You set your own schedule. No hourly floor, no ceiling.
  • "Plenty of people find it is not for them." Say that out loud, early.
  • The research reason this works: necessity-driven self-employment satisfies far less than opportunity-driven. Honesty filters out your week-two attrition before it costs you a training slot.
  • No urgency, no scarcity, no limited spots. Pressure recruits the desperate, and the desperate quit.
Done whenThe expectations message is a fixed step in the DM sequence, sent before the application link every time.
R4

Onboard on real doors, in three days

  • Replace the week-long class with three days of doors alongside someone good.
  • One onboarding path, written so a stranger could run it.
  • The person who trains them is the person who knocks with them. That is pillar 3 and it is also just the fastest way to train.
  • Core cycle time to measure: comment to first knock. That number governs how fast the whole network can grow.
Done whenComment to first knock is measured in days, not weeks, and you know the number.
R5

Plan for week two, because that is where they go

  • Week two is the kill zone. Treat it as a designed part of the process, not a surprise.
  • Build the week-two touchpoint: a named person, a ride-along, a specific conversation about the slow days.
  • Your first reel hook is literally "why do most new reps quit in week two" - answer it internally before you answer it publicly.
  • Compare two anonymized 90-day activity curves and find what the survivor did differently in days 8-14.
Done whenDay-90 survival improves against your own baseline, and you can name the change that moved it.
R6

Pay in recognition, and run the quarterly field day

  • Repost the two best rep posts every Thursday, named. For most of the roster, recognition is the pay.
  • Quarterly field day: half training, half recognition, filmed end to end. It solves retention and recruiting in one afternoon and produces a quarter of content.
  • It is training and recognition - not a ticketed product. The moment you sell a course about door-to-door you have changed businesses and taken on the entire earnings-claim regime.
Done whenThe first field day has happened and the footage is cut into the reel series.
R7

Recruit through your reps, not around them

  • Your best recruiting creators are already on your roster. Do not go looking for D2D influencers.
  • Whitelist recruiting ads from the rep's own handle, with written permission. Perceived first-hand experience converts better than the same message from the brand page.
  • Pay a bonus when someone they sourced completes a ride-along and onboards - outcome, not effort.
  • Second tier if you need more: local micro-creators in adjacent work - trades, delivery, gig driving.
Done whenMore than half of new onboards trace back to a rep, not to you.
R8

Track the two real numbers, ignore the rest

  • Ride-alongs booked per month. Applications are vanity - people apply to everything.
  • Day-90 survival. Not units sold, not headcount.
  • Coverage ratio below 2x means you are shrinking. Fix that before you fix anything else on the scoreboard.
Done whenBoth numbers appear on the Friday scoreboard and nothing else about recruiting does.

Recruiting guardrails

  • No entry fee, no kit purchase. Company-owned equipment, issued and returned. An entry fee wearing a costume is still an entry fee.
  • No income claims - not from you, not from a rep, not from an operator, not in a DM.
  • Never require posting or demand hours. Both are control signals against the 1099 classification.
  • Advancement never comes from recruiting. Promotion for bringing in an associate is the clearest marker of a pyramid-shaped incentive.
  • Reps may post the day, the route, a pitch, a bad door, why they took the work, team moments, their honest complaints. They may not post any dollar figure, check, statement or app screenshot, any range or "six figures", any promise about what someone else could earn, unblurred customer data, or unapproved carrier offers.
  • Run a takedown path. Someone checks tagged content weekly. A number goes up, you ask the same day, and you keep the record that you did.

06.1 / Carnegie, in full

The Empire of Business (1902, this text from the 1917 Doubleday printing) is out of copyright. The chapter below, The Road to Business Success, was a talk to young men at Curry Commercial College in Pittsburgh. It is the closest thing on this page to a direct address to a room full of new reps.

Verbatim - "The Road to Business Success," The Empire of Business

"And here is the prime condition of success, the great secret: concentrate your energy, thought, and capital exclusively upon the business in which you are engaged. Having begun in one line, resolve to fight it out on that line, to lead in it; adopt every improvement, have the best machinery, and know the most about it.

"The concerns which fail are those which have scattered their capital, which means that they have scattered their brains also. They have investments in this, or that, or the other, here, there, and everywhere. 'Don't put all your eggs in one basket' is all wrong. I tell you 'put all your eggs in one basket, and then watch that basket.' Look round you and take notice; men who do that do not often fail. It is easy to watch and carry the one basket. It is trying to carry too many baskets that breaks most eggs in this country. He who carries three baskets must put one on his head, which is apt to tumble and trip him up. One fault of the American business man is lack of concentration."

HereThis is the entire argument for the product roadmap in step B7, written in 1885. One carrier until the playbook exists. Two carriers plus one security line per cell, never eight. Carnegie is not saying focus is virtuous - he is saying scattered capital means scattered attention, and attention is the thing that actually runs out.
Verbatim - same chapter, on how a subordinate gets promoted

"I can give you the secret. It lies mainly in this: Instead of the question, 'What must I do for my employer?' substitute 'What can I do?' Faithful and conscientious discharge of the duties assigned you is all very well, but the verdict in such cases generally is that you perform your present duties so well that you had better continue performing them. Now, young gentlemen, this will not do. It will not do for the coming partners. There must be something beyond this. We make Clerks, Bookkeepers, Treasurers, Bank Tellers of this class, and there they remain to the end of the chapter. The rising man must do something exceptional, and beyond the range of his special department. He must attract attention."

HereThis is your operator selection criterion, and it is better than any interview question. You are not looking for the rep with the most units. You are looking for the one who did something outside the range of their own department - built a route nobody asked for, fixed a rebuttal for the whole team, noticed a serviceability gap. Carnegie's test is behavioural and it shows up on a ride-along.
Verbatim - same chapter, on saving as the qualifying signal

"There is one sure mark of the coming partner, the future millionaire: his revenues always exceed his expenditures. He begins to save early, almost as soon as he begins to earn. No matter how little it may be possible to save, save that little. Invest it securely, not necessarily in bonds, but in anything which you have good reason to believe will be profitable, but no gambling with it, remember. A rare chance will soon present itself for investment. The little you have saved will prove the basis for an amount of credit utterly surprising to you. Capitalists trust the saving young man. For every hundred dollars you can produce as the result of hard-won savings, Midas, in search of a partner, will lend or credit a thousand; for every thousand, fifty thousand. It is not capital that your seniors require, it is the man who has proved that he has the business habits which create capital."

HereRead the last sentence twice. It is not the capital, it is the proof of the habits that create capital. That is exactly what Gate 1 is: three consecutive profitable months is not a large sum of money, it is evidence of the habit. And note the closing rule of the same chapter - expenditure always within revenue - which is step B4 in nineteenth-century English.
Verbatim - "The Common Interest of Labour and Capital," an address to workingmen

"It is very unfortunate that the irresistible tendency of our age, which draws manufacturing into immense establishments, requiring the work of thousands of men, renders it impossible for employers who reside near to obtain that intimate acquaintance with employees which, under the old system of manufacturing in very small establishments, made the relation of master and man more pleasing to both. When articles were manufactured in small shops by employers who required only the assistance of a few men and apprentices, the employer had opportunities to know every one, to become well acquainted with each, and to know his merits both as a man and as a workman; and, on the other hand, the workman being brought into closer contact with his employer, inevitably knew more of his business, of his cares and troubles, of his efforts to succeed, and more important than all, they came to know something of the characteristics of the man himself. All this is changed.

"Thus the employees become more like human machines, as it were, to the employer, and the employer becomes almost a myth to his men. From every point of view this is a most regrettable result, yet it is one for which I see no remedy."

HereCarnegie could see the problem and said outright he had no fix for it. You do. The cell exists precisely so the owner never becomes a myth to the people selling for him - 8 to 15 reps is the size at which one accountable person still knows everyone's week. This passage is the strongest argument in this entire document for capping cell size and refusing the branch-office model.
Verbatim - "How to Win Fortune," on why salaried management loses

"These vast concerns cannot be successfully conducted by salaried employees. No great business of any kind can score an unusually brilliant, and permanent success which is not in the hands of practical men pecuniarily interested in its results. . . . In every branch of business this law is at work, and concerns are prosperous, generally speaking, just in proportion as they succeed in interesting in the profits a larger and larger proportion of their ablest workers."

HereThe whole owner-operator network, argued in 1890-something. An operator with their own entity and their own downside outperforms a salaried branch manager - not because of culture, but because of where the risk sits. It is also the reason override must be paid on production and never on headcount: the moment the operator's money comes from recruiting rather than from selling, they are no longer pecuniarily interested in the right thing.

06.2 / Rockefeller, in full

Random Reminiscences of Men and Events (Doubleday, 1909) is public domain in the United States. Rockefeller wrote it at seventy, mostly to answer his critics, which makes it unusually specific about mechanics.

Verbatim - Chapter III, on knowing your own numbers

"Then, again, we had the type of man who really never knew all the facts about his own affairs. Many of the brightest kept their books in such a way that they did not actually know when they were making money on a certain operation and when they were losing. This unintelligent competition was a hard matter to contend with. Good old-fashioned common sense has always been a mighty rare commodity. When a man's affairs are not going well, he hates to study the books and face the truth. From the first, the men who managed the Standard Oil Company kept their books intelligently as well as correctly. We knew how much we made and where we gained or lost. At least, we tried not to deceive ourselves."

HereNothing in this document has aged less. Most D2D operators cannot tell you their chargeback rate or their day-90 survival number, and the reason is the one Rockefeller names - when things are going badly, people stop opening the books. Three numbers, separately, always: written, installed, surviving at day 90. That is steps B2 and B4, and "we tried not to deceive ourselves" is the whole standard.
Verbatim - Chapter III, on where the company's success came from

"I ascribe the success of the Standard Oil Company to its consistent policy of making the volume of its business large through the merit and cheapness of its products. It has spared no expense in utilizing the best and most efficient method of manufacture. It has sought for the best superintendents and workmen and paid the best wages. It has not hesitated to sacrifice old machinery and old plants for new and better ones. It has placed its manufactories at the points where they could supply markets at the least expense."

HereNote what is absent: no mention of persuasion, motivation, or outselling anyone. It is all merit of the product, cost of delivery, quality of the people, and placement. "Placed its manufactories at the points where they could supply markets at the least expense" is serviceability-defined territory - step O5 - stated as a cost principle rather than a mapping trick.
Verbatim - Chapter IV, on distributed risk and self-insurance

"Here is an example of one of the ways in which we achieved certain economies and gained real advantage. Fires are always to be reckoned with in oil refining and storage, as we learned by dear experience, but in having our plants distributed all over the country the unit of risk and possible loss was minimized. No one fire could ruin us, and we were able thus to establish a system of insuring ourselves. Our reserve fund which provided for this insurance could not be wiped out all at once, as might be the case with a concern having its plants together or near each other."

HereThis is the single most useful paragraph on the page for your business. No one fire could ruin us. A network of small independent cells across different carriers and different states is structurally the same defence: one dealer agreement ending, one licence problem, one bad operator cannot take the whole company down. It is also the argument for the reserve in step B4 - the fund exists so that a bad quarter is an expense, not an emergency.
Verbatim - Chapter III, on argument and decision-making

"It is not always the easiest of tasks to induce strong, forceful men to agree. It has always been our policy to hear patiently and discuss frankly until the last shred of evidence is on the table, before trying to reach a conclusion and to decide finally upon a course of action. . . . It's a pity to get a man into a place in an argument where he is defending a position instead of considering the evidence. His calm judgment is apt to leave him, and his mind is for the time being closed, and only obstinacy remains."

HereUseful in two places. With operators, decide by evidence rather than by who pushed hardest - and notice when you have backed someone into defending a position, because from that point you are no longer getting their judgment. With yourself, it is a warning about the delete list in step B1: it is very easy to end up defending a process you introduced rather than considering whether it should exist.
Verbatim - Chapter II, on keeping your head after early success

"As our successes began to come, I seldom put my head upon the pillow at night without speaking a few words to myself in this wise: 'Now a little success, soon you will fall down, soon you will be overthrown. Because you have got a start, you think you are quite a merchant; look out, or you will lose your head - go steady.' These intimate conversations with myself, I am sure, had a great influence on my life. I was afraid I could not stand my prosperity, and tried to teach myself not to get puffed up with any foolish notions."

HereThe richest man in American history talked himself down from his own good months, on purpose, as a discipline. That is the Gate 4 stop rule stated as a personal habit rather than a policy - and it pairs exactly with Gates on success being a lousy teacher, two men a century apart naming the same failure mode.
Verbatim - Chapter II, on how he actually cold-called

"About this time I began to go out and solicit business - a branch of work I had never before attempted. I undertook to visit every person in our part of the country who was in any way connected with the kind of business that we were engaged in, and went pretty well over the states of Ohio and Indiana. I made up my mind that I could do this best by simply introducing our firm, and not pressing for immediate consignments. I told them that I represented Clark & Rockefeller, commission merchants, and that I had no wish to interfere with any connection that they had at present, but if the opportunity offered we should be glad to serve them, etc., etc.

"To our great surprise, business came in upon us so fast that we hardly knew how to take care of it, and in the first year our sales amounted to half a million dollars."

HereThe most useful paragraph on this page for an actual door. Rockefeller's own approach to canvassing was introduce, do not press - explicitly telling prospects he had no wish to displace whoever they were using, only to be available when the opportunity came. That is the opposite of every high-pressure D2D script in this industry, and it came from the man who ended up owning the trade. Put it next to hook 04 in your reel series and next to the rebuttal sheet from M3.
Verbatim - Chapter II, on refusing his best customer

"Not long after our concern was started our best customer - that is, the man who made the largest consignments - asked that we should allow him to draw in advance on current shipments before the produce or a bill of lading were actually in hand. We, of course, wished to oblige this important man, but I, as the financial member of the firm, objected, though I feared we should lose his business. . . . Naturally, he was very much disturbed at the possibility of losing our most valued connection, but I insisted and we stuck to our principles and refused to give the shipper the accommodation he had asked. What was our surprise and gratification to find that he continued his relations with us as though nothing had happened, and did not again refer to the matter. I learned afterward that an old country banker, named John Gardener, of Norwalk, O., who had much to do with our consignor, was watching this little matter intently, and I have ever since believed that he originated the suggestion to tempt us to do what we stated we did not do as a test, and his story about our firm stand for what we regarded as sound business principles did us great good."

HereTheir biggest account asked for an exception to the terms, and the whole thing turned out to be a test staged by someone watching from outside. This is the argument for step B3 - one published commission schedule, no discretionary haircut, ever. The first time you shave a payout for your top producer, everyone finds out, and what they learn is that the schedule is negotiable.
Verbatim - Chapter III, on selling direct to the user

"It gradually extended its services first to the large centres, and then to towns, and now to the smallest places, going to the homes of its customers, delivering the oil to suit the convenience of the actual users. . . . This plan of selling our products direct to the consumer and the exceptionally rapid growth of the business bred a certain antagonism which I suppose could not have been avoided, but this same idea of dealing with the consumer directly has been followed by others and in many lines of trade, without creating, so far as I recall, any serious opposition.

"This is a very interesting and important point, and I have often wondered if the criticism which centred upon us did not come from the fact that we were among the first, if not the first, to work out the problems of direct selling to the user on a broad scale."

HereStandard Oil was a door-to-door company. Tank wagons to the homes of customers in the smallest places, and Rockefeller believed the hostility toward his company came substantially from being first to do direct selling at scale. Worth remembering the next time somebody tells you knocking doors is a dying trade - and worth noticing that "to suit the convenience of the actual users" is a nineteenth-century install window.
Verbatim - Chapter III, on what a business is actually made of

"In speaking of the real beginning of the Standard Oil Company, it should be remembered that it was not so much the consolidation of the firms in which we had a personal interest, but the coming together of the men who had the combined brain power to do the work, which was the actual starting-point. Perhaps it is worth while to emphasize again the fact that it is not merely capital and 'plants' and the strictly material things which make up a business, but the character of the men behind these things, their personalities, and their abilities; these are the essentials to be reckoned with."

HereRead next to Carnegie on salaried employees and you have your operator selection standard from both of them: the asset is the person, not the territory and not the tooling. It is also the reason the Gate 4 stop rule exists - approving an operator you would have rejected at Gate 2 degrades the only thing the network is actually made of.
Verbatim - Chapter I, on borrowing before you need it

"I was always a great borrower in my early days; the business was active and growing fast, and the banks seemed very willing to loan me the money. About this time, when our great fire had brought up some new conditions, I was studying the situation to see what our cash requirements would be. We were accustomed to prepare for financial emergencies long before we needed the funds."

HereThe last sentence is step B4 in one line. Arrange the facility, size the reserve and know the number before the quarter where chargebacks land badly - because the terms you get when you do not need money are not the terms you get when you do.

Before you quote either of them publicly

  • Both men are contested figures, and your audience knows it. Standard Oil was broken up by the Supreme Court in 1911. Carnegie wrote warmly about workingmen and his company crushed the Homestead strike in 1892. Quoting the business mechanics is fine. Presenting either man as a moral model invites a fight you do not need on a recruiting channel.
  • Use them on Channel A, not Channel B. A founder's channel can carry a nineteenth-century business quote. A recruiting reel cannot carry anything that sounds like a lecture on thrift to people you are asking to work commission-only.
  • Carnegie's thrift material does not port at all. "Expenditure always within revenue" is sound advice for your company. Aimed at a 1099 contractor with no income floor, it reads as blaming them for what they earn. Keep it internal.
  • The 1889 "Wealth" essay is also public domain if you want more Carnegie, but it is about philanthropy and inheritance, not operations. Nothing in it belongs in this playbook.

06.3 / Rothschild, from the public domain record

Three sources feed this section, all out of copyright. John Reeves, The Rothschilds: The Financial Rulers of Nations (1887) - the standard English biography of the family, archived as not in copyright, and the source for the Mayer Amschel material below. Nathan Mayer Rothschild's sworn evidence to the House of Commons Committee of Secrecy on the Bank of England Charter, 1832. And Nathan's own 1824 business correspondence. Quotations are as printed, with obvious scanning errors in the 1887 text corrected.

Verbatim - Reeves, 1887, on Mayer Amschel's death, 13 September 1812

"Mayer Amschel Rothschild died on the 13th September, 1812, in his sixty-seventh year. Just before his decease he summoned his five sons to his bedside, and with his last breath, after giving them his blessing, enjoined them to remain faithful to the law of Moses, to remain united until the end, and to undertake nothing without having first consulted their mother. 'Observe these three points and you will soon be rich among the richest, and the world will belong to you' - a prediction that has been fulfilled to the letter."

The three points, separated out
  1. Remain faithful to the law of Moses. Keep the code you were raised under. Not a business tactic - the standard everything else is measured against.
  2. Remain united until the end. Five men, five cities, one house. The instruction that made a network out of five sons instead of five rivals.
  3. Undertake nothing without having first consulted their mother. No unilateral moves. Every significant decision passes through a fixed check outside the person making it.
Verbatim - Reeves, opening the next chapter, on whether it worked

"The dying injunctions of Mayer Amschel to his five sons were faithfully observed . . . even if filial obedience had not led his sons to live in unity together, their natural shrewdness would have at once pointed out to them the advantages which would follow from their combined action. But, be the motives what they may, it is a matter of history that the five sons, after their father's death, started business in five of the European capitals, each brother managing his own, but always acting on important occasions in concert with the others. The result of this union of aims and action was that they all rose simultaneously to fame and fortune, rising, too, with a rapidity which appears incredible."

HereTake the three in order. The first is a values statement - the same job your four pillars do in step M1, and note that the founder put it above the commercial instructions rather than after them. The second is your operator agreement: the terms exist so five independent operators stay one company once the money is large enough to pull them apart. The third is the one people skip - a standing check outside yourself before you act. You do not have a mother to consult; you have a scoreboard and a gate. That is what B2 and the Gate rules in section 03 are actually for.

And read Reeves's follow-up carefully, because it is the strongest part: he says the sons would have reached the same arrangement on shrewdness alone, obedience aside. The structure was not sentiment. It was correct, and it would have been correct for anyone.
Verbatim - Reeves, 1887, on where the business actually came from

"In all his business transactions he displayed remarkable honesty and integrity; so widely did he become known as the 'honest Jew' that his reputation spread through the surrounding provinces, and was largely the means of securing him fresh business. A man of his character has never lacked friends, and Mayer Amschel found many persons ready and anxious to recommend him and gain him new clients. Oppenheim, his old employer, was especially zealous in promoting the success of his former employe, and never allowed an opportunity to slip of saying a word in his favour."

HereThis is a referral engine described in 1887 language. Reputation spread through the provinces and was largely the means of securing him fresh business - not advertising, not persuasion, reputation doing the acquisition. And the person doing the most promoting was the employer he had left. Two things follow for you: the referral programme in step B6 is the oldest growth channel in this document, and how a rep leaves your organisation matters, because former people talk.
Verbatim - Reeves, 1887, on the years before any of it worked

"Frugal and economical in his habits, he was able to save a considerable portion of his salary, until he thought he possessed sufficient capital to make a start on his own account. He therefore left Oppenheim's service and set up in business for himself, dealing in old coins, bullion, and anything by which he thought he could make a profit. For some time it was a hard, uphill fight, and more than once the budding firm was in danger of collapse, but the untiring energy and honesty of its founder, triumphing over all difficulties, placed it on a sound basis and secured its future safety."

HereThe part the mythology leaves out. He worked for someone else first, saved out of a salary until he had a stake, went out on his own, and then nearly went under more than once. No moment of genius - energy and honesty applied through a stretch where it was not working yet. That is week two of a new rep's career, and it is the honest answer to your first reel hook. Put this passage in front of anyone who thinks the founding story of the richest family in Europe started with an advantage.
Verbatim - Reeves, 1887, on the founder's early trading

"His business was at the outset of a very mixed description, ranging from coins and curiosities, to bullion, bills of exchange, &c., but as his speculations, distinguished by cautious boldness, were almost invariably successful, he was soon in a position to abandon the business of a dealer in works of art for that of a banker and financier."

HereNote the sequence, because it is the opposite of the usual advice. He started mixed and narrowed as he proved out - not the reverse. That is your product roadmap: you hold a portfolio nationally and sell one or two lines locally, and you drop what does not qualify rather than adding what has not. "Cautious boldness" is a better description of Gate 1 than anything in section 03.
Verbatim - Reeves, 1887, on the job nobody else would take

"During the war in Spain the Duke of Wellington experienced great annoyance and embarrassment from the failure of the specie supplies, and the English Government were unable to assist him in his difficulty, as they could induce no bankers to assume the responsibility of conveying the money to Spain. At this juncture M. A. Rothschild stepped forward and offered to undertake the task for a pretty heavy commission. His offer was accepted, fortune smiled on his enterprise, and the money reached the Duke safely. This was a most profitable piece of business, as Rothschild is said to have cleared annually for eight years a sum of about £150,000. The success of this operation, and the punctuality which distinguished its execution, induced the Government to entrust the Rothschilds with the payment of the enormous subsidies to the various Continental princes, and this, again, still further increased the growing reputation and influence of the firm."

HereThe single most transferable paragraph about Mayer Amschel in the whole book. No other banker would take the job. It was unglamorous, physically risky logistics in a war zone, and that is exactly why the margin was there and why he asked a heavy commission without apology. Then note what actually compounded: not the fee - the punctuality. Executing an ugly job reliably is what got them the far larger subsidy business afterward.

Door-to-door is the ugly job in telecom sales. Everyone wants the residual; nobody wants the doors. Price it accordingly, execute it reliably, and the bigger contracts follow from the reliability rather than from the pitch.
Verbatim - Reeves, 1887, quoting Gudula Rothschild, who refused to leave the old house in the Judengasse

"Here I have seen," said she, "my sons grow rich and powerful, and, as I have not become conceited in my old age, I will leave them their good fortune, which would certainly forsake them, were I from pride to abandon my humble dwelling."

HereThe mother of the five, still living in the same street after her sons had become the richest family in Europe, on the grounds that pride would cost them the thing they had built. Put it beside Rockefeller talking himself down from his own good months. Two of the largest fortunes ever assembled, and in both households the same private discipline against believing your own run.
Verbatim - Reeves, 1887, quoting Nathan Mayer Rothschild on the Hesse-Cassel money

"The Prince of Hesse-Cassel gave my father his money; there was no time to lose, so he (my father) sent it me, and I put it to such good use that the Prince made me a present of all his wine and linen."

HereThe founding capital of the house was someone else's money, held in trust, handled well. The whole fortune turned on being the man a client would hand everything to while fleeing a war. That is what your holdback reserve and your published schedule are actually protecting - not the cash, the standing that lets people hand you things.
Verbatim - Nathan Mayer Rothschild, evidence to the Bank Committee, 1832

"This country in general is the Bank for the whole world . . . all transactions in India, in China, in Germany, in the whole world, are guided here, and settled through this country."

HereAsked under oath why the world's business ran through London, he credited not his own cleverness but the fact that London was where things settled. That is the position the back office in step B5 is building toward. An operator does not pay override for charisma; they pay because their business settles in your systems.
Verbatim - same testimony, describing the five houses working day to day

"I buy on the Exchange bills drawn from Liverpool, Manchester, New-castle and other places, and which come to every banker and merchant in London. I purchase £6,000 or £7,000, and sometimes £10,000 of those bills in a week, and I send them to the Continent to my houses; my houses purchase against them bills upon this country which are purchases for wine, wool and other commodities . . . [I]f there be not a sufficient supply of bills abroad on this country we are obliged to get gold from Paris, Hamburg and elsewhere."

HereThe network in plain mechanics, by the man running it, under questioning. Notice what is absent: no head office issuing orders, no chain of command. Each house buys in its own market and the flow between them is the advantage. Note the scale too - weekly volumes, not empires. Your version: each cell works its own serviceability footprint, and what moves between cells is information and standards, not instructions.
Verbatim - Nathan's 1824 letter on founding the Alliance Assurance Company

Writing to enlist government support, Nathan first set out what the venture was for: "The object of this Society is to promote all kinds of national industry, by affording facilities in the advancement of Capital, and to protect Commercial men and society in general by granting insurancies on shipping and every species of property exposed to risks. There are other ends, equally salutary, towards which the views of the Company will be directed, all tending to give an impetus to manufactures, and to attract and retain in their ports every branch of foreign commerce."

He then argued that this was simply where the world was already going: "This is . . . the policy . . . of the whole European Continent at the present moment: everywhere, efforts are [afoot] to introduce a spirit of commercial enterprise, to revive trade where it has languished, and to discover new channels in which it may be directed."

And only then came the point of the letter - that the incumbents lacked "that energy and those liberal extended views which are necessary, at the present day, to retain the advantages which they have hitherto monopolised, and I am sure I shall be borne out in the assertion that if insurancies are to be tied up by their old fashioned modes of thinking and acting, Establishments of a similar nature will arise in every part of the Continent, and will eventually wrest from their hands the business which they now conduct exclusively."

HereRead the letter as a whole and it is a template for asking a gatekeeper to let you in. He leads with what it does for everyone else - capital, protection, commerce - not with what it does for him. He then frames his entry as the direction things are already moving, so backing him is not a favour but alignment. Only at the end does he name the incumbents' weakness, and even that is phrased as an assertion he expects to be borne out rather than an attack.

That is the structure of every carrier conversation and every operator pitch you will have. And the substance is uncomfortably exact for your industry: incumbents holding a position by habit rather than merit - old fashioned modes of thinking and acting - lose it to leaner entrants arising everywhere at once. The agencies running a lease, a title ladder and a mandatory morning meeting are the monopolists in that sentence. He was the new entrant, and he was right.
Verbatim - on the Bank of England, 1832 testimony

"I feel the management and I know that it is good."

HereEleven words on how he judged a counterparty. Not the balance sheet, not the name - the management. Your operator approval standard and your carrier assessment in one line, and the same conclusion Rockefeller reached about character being the essential thing.
Verbatim - Reeves, 1887, on how he spent the money and the last years

"Although Mayer Rothschild is known to history merely as an eminent financier, money-making did not monopolize his time or thoughts. With all his engagements, he still managed to find leisure and opportunities for administering relief from his well-lined purse to his suffering fellow-mortals, and many tales are told of his benevolence and charity. He would often stop a poor, starving creature in the street, to place a coin in his hand and hurry away to prevent his face being recognized. He moreover exerted his influence to obtain an extension of the civil and political rights of the Jews, and the abolition of the tyrannical restrictions from which they suffered."

And on the handover: "Having established the firm on a solid basis, Mayer Amschel during the last years of his life left the management to a great extent in the hands of his sons, and devoted himself to work more congenial to his better nature."

HereTwo things worth taking. He hurried away so his face would not be recognised - the giving was not a marketing channel, which is precisely why it is in the biography seventy-five years later. And he handed the firm to his sons while he was still alive and able, rather than dying at the desk. Gate 2 in section 03 is the same test written as a business rule: if the cells only work when you are in them, you have not actually built anything transferable yet.
Verbatim - Reeves, 1887, quoting Heinrich Heine's memory of him in the streets of Frankfurt

"Old Rothschild, the founder of the reigning dynasty, was a noble soul, goodness and kindheartedness itself; a benevolent face with a pointed little beard; on his head a three-cornered hat, and his dress quiet and sober, if not poor. Thus would he go about in Frankfort, and frequently surrounded, as if holding a levee, by a crowd of poor people, to whom he would either give alms or address a kind word. If a row of beggars was met in the street, with cheerful and pleased countenances, one might be sure that Rothschild was in the habit of passing down that way."

HereThe richest man in Frankfurt, dressed "quiet and sober, if not poor," recognisable in the street by the mood of the people he had just walked past. Set it beside his wife refusing to leave the old house. Whatever else the family was, the founder's generation did not perform the money - and the fortune outlasted four more generations of people who did.

Family working maxims - paraphrased, because the originals are translations

The brothers' letters were written in German and Judendeutsch, so the English versions in circulation are modern translations. These are given as sense rather than quotation.

  • Spread the sugar first to catch the birds later. James's 1836 advice to his nephews on the Paris exchange: stop trying to profit on early trades and make brokers want to come to you, accepting sacrifices up front to become the default counterparty. Your referral programme and your day-7 call.
  • Work in pairs rather than singly. James's stated preference, repeatedly, over working alone. Your ride-along, and why a new rep is trained by the person who knocks with them.
  • Listen to everything and say little in reply. Salomon's coaching to his son before sensitive negotiations. Put it beside Rockefeller on arguing a man into defending a position.

The structure the five sons ran

Reeves, 1887, in full: "He left five sons, Anselm Mayer, Salomon, Nathan, James, and Carl, who soon after his death established a pentarchy, Anselm retaining command of the original house in Frankfort, whilst the others founded new branches in Vienna, London, Paris, and Naples respectively. By so doing they wonderfully increased the reputation and influence of the firm, and gradually gained that control over the different money markets which enabled them to augment their fortunes with such astounding certainty and rapidity."

He adds that the arrangement made the family history almost impossible to disentangle afterwards, "owing to the impossibility of ascertaining what part each played in the many gigantic operations undertaken and carried out conjointly by all" - which is itself the point. A network that works does not leave a clean record of who did what. The governing instrument was a partnership agreement rewritten every few years.

  • Locally autonomous, centrally accountable. Each house ran its own market; the agreements required the houses to report transactions to each other, weekly at first and later monthly.
  • Profits pooled and ploughed back. Unlike rivals who paid profits out to partners, the Rothschilds retained theirs, so capital compounded. Their combined capital in 1825 was roughly nine times that of their nearest competitor.
  • Terms did the governing. Disputes, succession and exit were handled by written clauses rather than supervision - including a penalty diverting a share to the poor if heirs sued.
  • Principals, not salaried managers. The houses were run by partners with capital at risk, and where distant cities were covered by salaried agents instead, the correspondence is full of friction about loyalty and incentive.

Here: independent cells, locally autonomous, held together by written terms and a reporting rhythm rather than a chain of command. The retained-profit point is step B4 - their advantage compounded because surplus went back into capacity instead of out to partners. And the salaried-agent friction is the strongest historical argument in this document for why an operator must carry their own downside.

Two cautions before you quote any of this publicly

  • Reeves warns about Rothschild mythology himself, in 1887. Before repeating one famous anecdote he stops to say: "In reproducing this anecdote, we wish it to be understood that we do not attach any value to it ourselves, our object only being to make the narrative as complete as possible. Many of the stories related of the Rothschilds are pure myths, for lively imaginations have placed a halo of romance about their history, as about that of every other person of notoriety, so that implicit credit cannot always be attached to the anecdotes told of the different members of the firm." He then repeats the story anyway and spends the following pages dismantling it, checking it against dates and geography until it collapses - noting that the version in circulation names a Rothschild "of whom no one has ever heard, as there was no Rothschild of that name living." Treat his reported conversations as period biography, not transcript, and quote the material above as "as recorded by his 1887 biographer." The same test kills the money-supply line everyone posts: it has no primary source at all, it lives in conspiracy literature, and it is already on your do-not-publish list at 06.8. You no longer need it - you have the deathbed instruction and the sworn testimony instead.
  • The 1887 book is of its time. Its opening chapter is an extended commentary on Jews and commerce that mixes admiration with the prejudices of the period, and it would not read well beside your logo. The biographical and business chapters are the usable part. Cite the book by name and year, quote only the passages above, and do not send anyone to chapter one.

Tier B from here down - short quotes, each from a named occasion

"Control your expenses better than your competition."

Sam Walton - one of his ten rules, Made in America, 1992

The idea

He called this the one place a competitive advantage is always available, and said Walmart could make real mistakes for years and still recover because it ran leaner than whoever it was fighting. Cost discipline was the margin of error that let him be wrong and survive it.

HereA cell with no lease survives a quarter that a leased office does not. Every deletion in B1 buys you the right to be wrong once.

"Only when the tide goes out do you discover who's been swimming naked."

Warren Buffett - used repeatedly in his letters to Berkshire shareholders

The idea

Thin reserves and optimistic accounting are invisible while conditions are good and fatal the moment they turn. He holds enough cash that a bad year is an opportunity rather than an emergency, and refuses business that only works if nothing goes wrong.

HereYour holdback and chargeback exposure exactly. Size the reserve for the cell count you will have in twelve months. That is B4, and it is the named way this business dies.

"Reputation is a fire which, once kindled, may be preserved."

Warren Buffett has made this point many ways; the specific framing above is the older proverb he echoes. His own best-known version - that a reputation takes twenty years to build and five minutes to ruin - runs longer than this page will quote.

The idea

His practical instruction to managers is the useful part: lose money for the firm and he will be understanding, lose a shred of reputation and he will be ruthless. Reputation is treated as a hard operating constraint, not a soft value.

HereThis is why the compliance surface in B9 is not on the delete list, and why the takedown path in section 04 has a named owner. One rep posting a check screenshot is a reputational cost you cannot buy back at any price.

"Simple can be harder than complex."

Steve Jobs - BusinessWeek interview, 1998

The idea

You have to work hard to get your thinking clean enough to make something simple, and the payoff is that a simple thing can be moved. Returning to Apple in 1997 he cut a sprawling product line to four machines - two consumer, two pro - and that subtraction is what saved the company.

HereOne commission schedule. One override level. One or two products per cell. One series banner. Cuts you have to keep making, because complexity grows back.

"Boil things down to their fundamental truths and reason up from there."

Elon Musk - describing first-principles thinking, TED interview, 2013. Tesla's revenue is below this section's band; included by request.

The idea

His stated method is to reason from physics rather than by analogy - analogy means doing what everyone else does with slight variation, which is fine until the thing everyone does is wrong. He applied it to battery cost by pricing the raw materials rather than accepting the market price of a pack.

HereApply it to the two inherited assumptions this document already deletes: an office needs a lease, and reps need a manager. Neither survives being priced from first principles. "An office" is a person, a roster and a serviceability list.

Sinegal's rule, and why it matters more than it looks

Costco caps its markup - roughly 14 percent on branded goods, a little more on its own label - and refuses to raise it even when the market would clearly bear it. Not generosity. The cap is the reason members renew, and a rule broken once quietly stops being a rule. Meanwhile the membership fee is the profit engine: predictable recurring revenue underneath a low-margin transactional business.

Here: the published commission schedule with no discretionary haircut is your markup cap - shave a payout once and the schedule stops being trusted, permanently. Monitoring RMR plus the water filter cycle is your membership fee. That is B8, and the whole reason ADT moved up the roadmap.

06.5 / Management

"Success is a lousy teacher."

Bill Gates - The Road Ahead, 1995

The idea

Success seduces capable people into believing they cannot lose, and the useful information sits in complaints instead. In a later book he made the companion point plainly: your unhappiest customers are where the learning is, so build a mechanism that carries their complaints to you undiluted.

HereBoth halves are already steps. The day-7 call (M2) is the mechanism. The Gate 4 stop rule (O7) is the guard against a good year - the pressure to approve weak operators always arrives right after a strong quarter.

"We hire smart people so they can tell us what to do."

Steve Jobs - remarks widely circulated from Apple internal footage, mid-1990s

The idea

His argument was that hiring good people and then handing them instructions destroys the reason you hired them. Apple ran with one profit-and-loss statement and deep collaboration between teams rather than divisions with their own scoreboards.

HereAn operator who needs your approval to run their week is a branch manager you are paying override to. Gate 2 tests this directly - two cells hitting survival without your daily involvement. If they only work when you are in them, you have one cell and two branches.

"Walk out of a meeting as soon as it is obvious you aren't adding value."

Elon Musk - productivity email to Tesla employees, 2018 (widely republished)

The idea

The same email told staff to drop large and frequent meetings, communicate by the shortest path rather than through a chain of command, and treat rules as guidance that common sense can override. He called staying in a useless meeting the rude act, not leaving one.

HereYour delete list already includes the daily morning meeting. Expect to re-add roughly one thing - likely a single weekly touchpoint, not daily and not mandatory, because mandatory attendance is a control signal against your 1099 classification.

Bezos on decisions and team size - paraphrased from the shareholder letters

  • Two-pizza teams. If two pizzas cannot feed a team, it is too big. Past a certain size, communication overhead beats autonomy.
  • Type 1 and type 2 doors. Some decisions are one-way and deserve slow deliberation. Most are two-way doors you can walk back through, and treating those as one-way is how a fast company turns slow.
  • Disagree and commit. You do not need consensus to move. Say plainly that you disagree, then back the decision fully instead of relitigating it for a month.
  • Roughly 70 percent of the information you wish you had is the right point to decide. Waiting for 90 means being slow.

Here: a cell of 8 to 15 reps is a two-pizza team, and that is not a coincidence. Your one-way doors are carrier agreements, licensing and operator approvals. A script, a route, a hook, a payout cadence - all two-way. Decide those in a day.

Walton on partnership and on being on the ground - paraphrased from his memoir

  • Share the profits and treat people as partners. He wrote that his biggest regret was not putting store staff into profit sharing sooner, and that people closest to the customer behave like owners only if they are treated like owners.
  • Listen to the people on the floor. They know the most, and the job of everyone above them is to push authority down rather than pull information up.
  • He visited stores relentlessly - not to inspect, but for the questions he could only ask standing in one.

Here: published economics and no discretionary haircut are how you honour the partnership in writing rather than in a speech. The ride-along is your store visit - the only place you learn what actually happens at a door.

Costco's retention lever, and the one you do not have

Sinegal's case for paying warehouse staff well above sector was operating, not moral: turnover is expensive, and a workforce that stays gets faster, sells more, and makes fewer costly errors. Costco's retention has been a competitive advantage for decades and the cost is repaid in throughput.

You cannot use his lever. Wages, benefits and hours are all off the table for a 1099 contractor. The same economics apply through different instruments: territory quality, training that transfers, a person who knocks with them, and recognition. The 28-reps-a-week treadmill in section 04 is what turnover costs at scale. Every point of day-90 survival is a point you do not have to recruit.

06.6 / Marketing

"Marketing is about values."

Steve Jobs - internal talk launching the Think Different campaign, 1997

The idea

The world is noisy and nobody gets to explain themselves at length, so a company has to decide what it stands for and say that instead of listing features. His example was a shoe company selling a commodity that never talks about the product and instead honours the people who use it. Same shoes, different meaning.

HereThe exact argument for selling control instead of income. Frontier fiber is a commodity - identical speeds, identical promo, identical install window at every door. You cannot win on an offer that is not yours. The four pillars in M1 are the only part of your pitch a competitor cannot copy by cutting a price.

"Great companies are built on great products."

Elon Musk - posted publicly, 2013

The idea

Tesla ran for years with essentially no advertising budget, on the argument that money spent persuading people is money not spent on the thing being sold. Demand came from the product, from owners talking, and from events that earned coverage rather than buying it.

HereYour product is not the fiber - it is the experience of being a rep here and the experience of being a customer here. Both are things you actually control, and both are what M2 and B6 are built to improve.

Walton on where to compete - paraphrased

Walmart did not open in glamorous markets. It saturated outward from a distribution centre into small towns the large chains judged too small to bother with, moving into contested ground only later. Logistics drew the map, not ambition.

Here: a serviceability-defined cell, forty years early. Draw territory from carrier footprint data, not from where you would like to work (O5), and saturate a cell before opening the next.

Bezos on customers versus competitors - paraphrased

He preferred customer obsession to competitor obsession on the grounds that competitors never ask you for anything while customers are permanently, usefully dissatisfied. Paired with working backwards: write the announcement and the customer's experience first, then build back to whatever makes it true.

Here: before opening a cell or adding a product, write the recruiting post and the install experience as if they exist. If you cannot write them convincingly, you are not ready to build. Note that Jobs, Bezos, Walton and Rockefeller independently land on the same instruction - start at the person who uses the thing and work backwards.

06.7 / Advertising

"Advertising is the price you pay for having an unremarkable product."

Attributed to Jeff Bezos - widely repeated, no single documented setting. Use as attributed, not as documented.

The idea

Amazon's early growth ran on selection, price, delivery speed and reviews rather than paid persuasion. The claim is not that advertising never works - it is that paid spend behind a product nobody talks about is a permanent tax, and paid spend behind one they do talk about is a multiplier.

HereYour advertising budget is mostly not advertising. It is the install that goes right, the rep who answers the phone in week three, the referral payout on surviving accounts, and the review four streets over.

What these companies actually did with paid media

  • Costco advertises almost not at all. No traditional ad budget to speak of. The money that would have been ad spend is left in the price.
  • Walmart replaced promotional advertising with a promise. Everyday low prices meant no weekly circulars manufacturing urgency, which cut a recurring cost line permanently.
  • Tesla ran on earned attention. No ad budget for years; product reveals, referral programs and owner word of mouth did the work paid media would have done.
  • Apple concentrated rather than spread. Few messages run hard, the agency in the room weekly, Jobs personally in the review - not many messages tested thinly.
  • Amazon let customers write the copy. Reviews and word of mouth did persuasion a brand would otherwise have bought.

The pattern, and your version: all five spent on the experience and let the audience distribute it. That is the Creator Corps in a sentence. Run organically first, whitelist only what already performs, kill anything flat in 72 hours, never pay to amplify something nobody engaged with for free. Concentrate like Apple: one banner, twelve hooks, in order.

06.8 / Limits

Where this does not port

  • Every one of them owned their product and employed their people. You do neither yet. Advice on customers, focus, subtraction and reserves transfers cleanly. Advice on culture, hours and hiring mostly does not.
  • Musk's hardcore culture is unavailable to you and running at it anyway is the fastest way to hand a regulator your classification. Take the algorithm and the meeting rules. Leave the hours.
  • Walton's Saturday morning meeting was a control ritual over employees - and it rebuilds the culture your brand is positioned against.
  • Costco's retention lever is compensation. Off the table. Do not approximate it with anything that looks like wages.
  • Buffett is a capital allocator. His discipline applies to surplus you already have. Reading it as a growth playbook will make you passive.
  • Founder-as-lightning-rod does not scale down. A national brand absorbs a bad week. An operation recruiting inside driving distance does not.

Do not publish these

  • Any founder quote used as an income claim by proxy. Fine on Channel A. Never where a recruit can see it.
  • "Stay hungry, stay foolish." Jobs said it at Stanford in 2005 while quoting the Whole Earth Catalog's sign-off. Attributing it to him as original is wrong and someone will know.
  • Think Different campaign copy. Agency-written and copyrighted. Do not reproduce it or attribute it to Jobs.
  • The customer who wanted a faster horse. Attributed to Henry Ford with no primary source in anything he wrote.
  • The Rothschild money-supply line and its Nathan variant. Both fabricated, both unsourced, both at home in conspiracy literature. Covered in full at 06.3 - where the real, sworn, public-record Rothschild material is, and it is better.
  • The "1 percent of a hundred men's effort" line attributed to Rockefeller does not appear anywhere in Random Reminiscences and has no primary source. It is the most-quoted thing he never wrote.
  • Anything that sounds like a modern motivational post. If a nineteenth-century industrialist sounds like a caption, someone wrote it recently. Test before publishing: can you name the book, letter or speech and the year? If not, drop it.
